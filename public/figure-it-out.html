By York Sims, Jr.

Figure It Out

Twelve rules for building something from nothing.

Twelve rulesThe Milk Crate CodePDF and paperback

When I was four, my dad couldn't afford a real hoop. He knocked the bottom out of a milk crate, nailed it to the back of our house and handed me the ball. “Here. Figure it out.”

I've been living by that sentence ever since. This book breaks it into twelve rules. Each chapter starts with something that happened when I was a kid and follows it to where it showed up again when I was grown, and each one ends with a short exercise for you to do on paper.

I wrote it like we're sitting at my desk. Plain and straight, with receipts.

“You can teach yourself anything if you’re willing to be bad at it first.”

From the introduction

Who it's for

  • Anyone starting over with less than they need, whether that's after a setback, after sports, or with nothing handed down.
  • Parents who want to give their kids a better script than the one they got.

“Your crate is sitting somewhere right now, looking ordinary, looking like not enough. It is enough.”

From chapter 1, Figure It Out.

My dad came around the side of the house carrying a milk crate in one hand and a hammer in the other, and nothing about the man said a tender moment was coming.

I was four years old. For weeks I had been begging for a basketball goal with the persistence only a four-year-old owns, because a four-year-old does not yet know that money is a real thing with real edges. All I knew was that basketball existed and I needed to be inside it.

Let me hand you the context I didn’t have then. This was Clarksville, Arkansas, a town of about 5,000 people where everybody knew exactly what everybody else didn’t have. We were broke. Not sitcom broke, where the family drives one older car and the whole crisis wraps up in 22 minutes. Broke for real, where a store-bought basketball goal, the pole, the backboard, the actual orange rim, was as realistic as buying a helicopter. Some parents would have said “maybe for Christmas” and let the lie do the babysitting. My dad didn’t traffic in that kind of lie.

What he did instead was what he always did. He looked at the problem, assessed what he had available, and engineered a solution from nothing. A milk crate was available. He knocked the bottom out of it. He nailed it to a piece of plywood on the back of the house, high enough to mean something. Then he stepped back, checked the work the way he checked all work, and gave me the entire ceremony in two sentences.

“There’s your basketball goal. Now quit bothering me.”

No speech about believing in myself. No card, no hug, no slow-motion moment where the music swells. A crate, a wall, and a screen door closing behind him.

Here’s what you need to understand: I did not feel cheated. I felt rich. I ran at that thing like it was Madison Square Garden. And the crate turned out to be a mean teacher from day one. It had no net to slow the ball down and whisper sweet things to you, no backboard glass to forgive a flat shot. The ball went through clean or it came back at your face with an attitude. So I learned to shoot it clean. Every day after school. Every summer morning. Until the dark made the rim a rumor and somebody hollered me inside. And every rim I met for the rest of my life was more generous than the one I learned on.

A crate doesn’t care that you’re four, either. That’s the other thing about mean teachers: they treat you like the real thing from the first rep. There was no adjustable height, no beginner setting, no version of that rim scaled down to a child. It hung where he nailed it, the same height on day one and day one thousand, and every make had to be earned at full difficulty. Kids are supposed to get things softened for them. Mine never was, and I’m convinced that’s half of why the game took root so deep.

Now the part that took me 30 years to fully see. A milk crate is not built to take a basketball for hours a day. About every other week, mine would crack and give out from the abuse. And every time it did, without an announcement, without a lecture about being careful with things, a new crate would be up on that plywood. He was not a man who said “I love you” on a schedule. He said it in nails. He recognized the fire in our eyes, and he fed it, one milk crate at a time. He never said any of that out loud. But he built me the hoop.

Understand, the crate wasn’t a one-off. The man engineered everything. The domino table where he held court sat on top of the storm cellar. When we needed baseball bats, he carved them out of two-by-fours. If a thing was needed and the money wasn’t there, the thing got built out of whatever was there. Watching that, week after week, installs something in a kid that no store can sell you.

If I stayed inside too long, got underfoot, started whining about being bored, the instruction never changed: “Boy, take your ass outside and shoot on the goal I put up.”

And if you had asked him for the philosophy underneath all of it, the entire instruction manual was one sentence long.

“Here. Figure it out.”

I’ve been figuring it out ever since.
